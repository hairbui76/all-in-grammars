# Connectors of Emphasis

> [!abstract] In one line
> `In fact`, `indeed`, `actually` and `as a matter of fact` reinforce, correct or gently contradict what has just been said.

## In fact — two uses

**1. Strengthening the idea just stated.**

- I don't like him. **In fact**, I can't stand him.

**2. Stating something contrary to the previous idea** (often after `but`).

- People think I don't like him, but **in fact** he's a good friend.
- You called him Pete. **In fact**, he is Peter.

## Indeed — strengthening

`Indeed` also adds force to an idea that has already been expressed.

- Our results this year have been satisfactory. **Indeed**, they are the best results we have had for several years.

## Actually / As a matter of fact

Used when the speaker introduces a more accurate detail, or disagrees mildly and politely.

- I'm going to stay with someone I know in Paris. **Actually** / **As a matter of fact**, he's my cousin.
- A: I didn't like the singer very much. B: **Actually**, I thought she was excellent.

## Notes & exceptions

- `Actually` in English does NOT mean "at present" — it means "in reality".
- `As a matter of fact` softens a correction and is common in polite disagreement.
- Punctuation follows the general rule → see [[Sentence Connector Punctuation]].

## Contrast with

- [[Connectors of Contrast]] — `however` opposes; `in fact` corrects or intensifies
- [[Connectors of Addition]] — adds a separate point instead of reinforcing the same one
