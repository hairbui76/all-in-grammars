# Phrasal Verb Structure

> [!abstract] In one line
> A phrasal verb is a verb combined with one or two particles whose meaning cannot be worked out from the individual words.

## Form

`V + particle` — two-word verb
`V + adverb + preposition` — three-word verb

- `put` + `off` → **put off** = postpone
- `put` + `up` + `with` → **put up with** = tolerate

## When to use

- Phrasal verbs belong to ordinary spoken and written English; the single-word equivalents (`postpone`, `tolerate`, `investigate`) are usually more formal.
- The particle changes the meaning of the verb completely, so a phrasal verb must be learned as one vocabulary item, not as verb + preposition.
- One base verb can produce many unrelated phrasal verbs — compare `take off`, `take after`, `take on`, `take over`, `take up`.

## Examples

- Tonight's concert will be **put off** until next week.
- I can't **put up with** her another day, she never stops complaining.
- The woman **accused** the boy **of** breaking her radio.
- A fire **broke out** in the hospital last night.

## Notes & exceptions

- The particle may be an adverb (`blow up`, `turn on`) or a preposition (`look after`, `count on`). This difference decides whether the object can be separated → see [[Separable Phrasal Verbs]] and [[Inseparable Phrasal Verbs]].
- Many phrasal verbs have two or more distinct meanings: `break up` = end a relationship / start laughing; `make up` = invent, apply cosmetics, become friends again.
- A phrasal verb can be turned into a noun: `bring up` → *upbringing*; `break down` → *breakdown*.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ I postponed off the meeting. → ✅ I **put off** the meeting.
- ❌ He put up her behaviour with. → ✅ He **put up with** her behaviour.
