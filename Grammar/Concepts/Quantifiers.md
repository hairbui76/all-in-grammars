# Quantifiers

> [!abstract] In one line
> Determiners that say **how much** or **how many** — the choice depends on the countability of the noun.

## Form

| With countable plural | With uncountable | With both |
|---|---|---|
| `many`, `a few`, `few`, `several`, `a number of`, `a great many`, `both`, `each*`, `every*` | `much`, `a little`, `little`, `a great deal of`, `a large amount of` | `some`, `any`, `no`, `none of`, `a lot of`, `lots of`, `plenty of`, `most`, `all`, `enough` |

\* `each` and `every` take a **singular** countable noun.

`a number of + plural noun + plural verb` · `the number of + plural noun + singular verb`

## When to use

- `many / much` are most natural in questions and negatives; in positive statements prefer `a lot of`.
- `few / little` = a negative idea (not enough); `a few / a little` = a positive idea (some, enough).
- `some` in offers and requests expecting *yes*; `any` in negatives, most questions, and after `if`.
- `no + noun` = an emphatic negative; `none of + the/my + noun` stands alone.

## Examples

- There isn't **much time** left, so hurry.
- **A lot of students** applied, but **few** were accepted.
- Would you like **some tea**?
- I don't have **any money** on me.
- **A number of** guests **were** late, though **the number of** complaints **was** small.
- **All (of) the children** enjoyed the trip.

## Notes & exceptions

- Before a determiner (`the`, `my`, `this`) most quantifiers need `of`: `many of the students`, `most of my friends`, `all of them`.
- Without a determiner, no `of`: `most students`, `all children`.
- `each of / one of / every one of + plural noun + singular verb` → see [[Subject Verb Agreement]].
- `little` and `few` alone sound formal; everyday English uses `not much` / `not many`.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ I have many homeworks. → ✅ I have **a lot of homework**.
- ❌ Much of people came. → ✅ **Many of the** people came.
- ❌ A number of student is absent. → ✅ A number of **students are** absent.
