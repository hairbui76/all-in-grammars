# Which Referring to a Whole Clause

> [!abstract] In one line
> `which` after a comma can refer back to the whole idea of the preceding clause, not to a single noun — and `that` can never do this.

## Form

`Clause, which + comment`

Here `which` = "the fact that ...".

## When to use

- To attach a comment or reaction to a complete statement already made.
- The comma is obligatory; the clause is always non-defining.

## Examples

- Jim passed his driving test. This surprised everybody. → Jim passed his driving test, **which surprised everybody**. (`which` = the fact that Jim passed his driving test)
- Sheila couldn't come to the party, **which was a pity**.
- The weather was very good, **which we hadn't expected**.
- I had paid all the money before I received the goods. It was stupid of me. → I had paid all the money before I received the goods, **which was stupid of me**.

## Common mistakes

- ❌ Jim passed his driving test, that surprised everybody. → ✅ ..., **which** surprised everybody.
- ❌ Jim passed his driving test which surprised everybody. (no comma changes the meaning to a defining clause about the test)

## Contrast with

- [[Non-Defining Relative Clauses]] — `which` referring to one specific noun
- [[Connective Relative Clauses]] — `which`/`whom` after a quantifier such as `all of`, `most of`
